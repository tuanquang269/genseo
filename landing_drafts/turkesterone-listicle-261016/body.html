01

The human evidence is small

Plant ecdysteroids are discussed in sports nutrition. The key question is not what a compound is claimed to do, but what controlled human research has measured.

That human record is still short. A 2024 review describes interest in ecdysteroids, while noting that evidence for this compound remains limited.

Review · Nutrients 2024

02

One acute study found no effect

A preliminary human study gave multiple acute doses of the compound to 11 healthy men and checked blood IGF-1 and resting metabolic rate before, three hours after, and 24 hours after ingestion.

The authors reported no significant condition, time, or interaction effects. This was an acute study, not a test of long-term muscle growth or performance.

Human study · Harris et al., Muscles 2024

03

IGF-1 did not reach significance

The same study explored serum IGF-1, a marker sometimes used in discussions of muscle biology. The reported analyses did not find a statistically significant effect.

A non-significant rise is not proof of an anabolic benefit. The study also did not measure added muscle over a training block.

Human study · IGF-1 result reported without significance

04

Related compounds differ

Some human discussion centers on ecdysterone, a related ecdysteroid, rather than the compound in this guide. Related compounds can inform a research question, but they are not interchangeable evidence.

Ingredient identity matters. A result for ecdysterone should not be presented as a result for this product without a direct trial.

Evidence distinction · Nutrients 2024

05

Animal data stays preclinical

Plant ecdysteroid research also includes laboratory and animal models. Those experiments can suggest mechanisms worth testing, but they do not establish an effect in lifters.

The 2025 stress-resilience paper cited here studied Caenorhabditis elegans, not people. It did not test this product, human muscle growth, or training performance.

Animal model · C. elegans, IJMS 2025

06

The product has no matching trial

The acute study tested the compound, but it did not test the Wellness Nest formula shown after this bridge. A product label and a research intervention are not automatically the same thing.

We found no published human trial of this exact product in the sources reviewed. That is an evidence gap, not evidence of either benefit or harm.

Evidence gap · exact-product trial not found

07

Consistency is still untested

The available human study looked at short-term measurements after ingestion. It did not test a repeated training routine, strength progression, recovery across sessions, or long-term safety.

For lifters, that missing time horizon is important. Do not turn a short observation into a promise about months of training.

Study limitation · no long-term training outcome
