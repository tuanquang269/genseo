01

Know the extract

This is a plant compound from the ecdysteroid family, most often sourced from Ajuga turkestanica. It is marketed as a natural alternative to anabolic steroids for muscle support.

A 2024 review in Nutrients covers this compound alongside the related compound ecdysterone, both from plant sources. The two are related but not identical, so check which one a label actually names.

Review, Todorova et al., Nutrients 2024

02

Check the label, not the ad

Labels vary by brand. A pack can name an extract and a capsule count without printing a milligram amount or a Supplement Facts panel at all.

Read the front of the pack yourself before you buy. If a milligram number, a serving size, or a day-supply is not printed on it, that number does not exist for that product.

How to read any supplement pack

03

One small study found no effect

In a preliminary study of 11 healthy men, researchers measured IGF-1 and resting metabolic rate before, 3 hours after, and 24 hours after a single dose.

The result: no statistically significant change in IGF-1, metabolic rate, or lipid and carbohydrate metabolism compared with placebo. IGF-1 trended up but did not reach significance.

Human study, Harris et al., Muscles 2024

04

A single dose is not weeks of use

The Muscles 2024 study tested a single dose with a 24-hour follow-up. It did not test daily use over weeks, and it did not measure strength or muscle size.

So an acute hormone or metabolic marker result does not tell you what months of daily use would do. That question has not been answered in humans yet.

Study limitation, no long-term training outcome

05

Two names are not one compound

The 2024 Nutrients review discusses both compounds together because they share a plant family, but they are chemically distinct molecules with separate evidence bases.

Most of the published animal and lab research on this compound class used ecdysterone, not this specific compound. Read which one a cited study actually used before assuming it applies to the extract on the shelf.

Evidence distinction, Todorova et al., Nutrients 2024

06

No combined-formula trial exists

A 2025 lab study in IJMS tested maral root extract, which contains this compound among other phytoecdysteroids, in the roundworm C. elegans, not in people.

We found no published human trial of a multi-ingredient capsule combining this extract with ashwagandha, tongkat ali, colostrum, and black pepper extract. A combined formula rests on each ingredient's own separate evidence, not a tested combination.

Literature check, no combined-formula human trial found
