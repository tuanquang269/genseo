NMN has clearer human trial evidence for raising blood NAD than the oral NAD+ evidence reviewed here, but that does not establish longer life or superiority in a head-to-head trial. ## What is the difference between NMN and NAD+?

**NMN is a precursor that the body can convert into NAD+; NAD+ is the coenzyme itself.** They are connected, not interchangeable names for the same ingredient. A randomized NMN study describes NAD+ as a redox cofactor involved in metabolism and a substrate for enzymes involved in processes including DNA repair. That biological role explains research interest; it does not prove that buying a supplement improves those processes in a particular person. [Source: randomized NMN trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35479740/).

The shopping problem is that a front label can describe a goal rather than the substance inside. Treat “NAD+ support” as a prompt to inspect the ingredient panel. Ask whether the product contains NMN, NAD+ itself, or another named ingredient. Do not compare bottles until that question has an answer.

Keep the route of administration separate, too. An oral capsule is not an infusion. A study of an intravenous product does not establish what happens after swallowing a different product. Likewise, a paper studying NMN is not automatically evidence for a capsule containing NAD+.

This comparison concerns oral supplements. It is not advice to seek an infusion, combine products, or diagnose a deficiency from tiredness. Its practical question is narrower: which claims can a buyer trace back to human evidence?

## NMN: what did the human trials actually measure?

**The strongest straightforward finding in the trials below is a rise in blood NAD-related measurements—not proof of slower aging.** Two placebo-controlled studies give useful examples of what was tested and what remains unanswered.

In a randomized, double-blind study of **30 healthy participants over 12 weeks**, investigators reported increased blood NAD+ with NMN and no obvious adverse effects within the study conditions. The paper describes NMN as a practical way to increase NAD+ in humans. Its size and duration should stay attached to that conclusion: this was not a decades-long assessment of longevity or a study of every retail NMN formulation. [Study and abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35479740/).

A separate randomized, double-blind study enrolled **80 healthy middle-aged adults for 60 days**. Blood NAD concentrations increased in the NMN groups compared with baseline and placebo. Researchers also reported improvements in a walking test and some self-reported health measures, while the insulin-resistance measure did not differ significantly between NMN and placebo. The study reported no safety issues under its monitoring conditions. [Study and abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36482258/).

That mix of findings matters. It would be selective to quote a favorable walking result while implying that every metabolic measure improved. It would also be wrong to turn the absence of a short-term safety signal into a guarantee of indefinite safety.

## Oral NAD+: what evidence should buyers ask for?

**Ask for human research on the exact oral formulation and the outcome being advertised.** The evidence reviewed for this article does not justify saying that oral NAD+ is universally ineffective—or that it reliably outperforms NMN.

One relevant source is a double-blind trial reported as a conference abstract in *Innovation in Aging*. Participants took oral NAD+ or placebo and completed cognitive testing at baseline and after **28 days**. After adjustment for baseline scores, the authors reported **no significant between-group differences**. Their conclusion was that NAD+ did not improve cognition more than placebo in that study. [Published conference abstract](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12762572/).

Read that result at its actual scale. It is a small, short report about cognition, not a direct NMN-versus-NAD+ comparison. The abstract does not provide a blood-NAD result that can be lined up against the NMN trials above. Its participant totals are also internally inconsistent, so this article does not rely on its stated enrollment count.

A positive change from baseline is not enough to show that a supplement caused the change. If the placebo group improves too, the between-group comparison becomes essential. The oral NAD+ abstract illustrates why “participants improved” can be an incomplete sales claim.

For a product described as liposomal or otherwise specially formulated, request evidence for that formulation rather than assuming the delivery term settles the question. A delivery claim, an absorption claim, a blood-marker claim, and a health-outcome claim are different propositions. Each needs evidence appropriate to what is being promised.
