## What does the turkesterone evidence actually show?

**The turkesterone studies reviewed here do not establish a reliable muscle-building benefit.** An acute experiment measured short-term biological responses; a separate supplement experiment measured outcomes over several weeks. Neither is a direct comparison with shilajit.

In a [preliminary acute turkesterone investigation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40757520/), eleven healthy men completed measurements before and after supplementation. The researchers assessed insulin-like growth factor-1, resting metabolic rate, and measures of fuel metabolism. They reported no statistically significant condition, time, or interaction effects for those outcomes.

That matters because a proposed mechanism is not the same as a demonstrated training result. A discussion about an anabolic signal cannot substitute for evidence that participants gained muscle or improved a meaningful performance measure. This experiment did not establish long-term muscle growth. Its authors called for further work rather than presenting a settled benefit.

A [four-week randomized, double-blind study of a turkesterone-containing supplement](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12379694/) involved 24 participants. It found no significant differences between treatment and placebo in changes in body composition, handgrip strength, mood, or sleep quality. The authors explicitly noted the small sample and short treatment period.

Read that result narrowly. It does not prove that every possible turkesterone preparation can never have an effect. It also does not justify promising a benefit while waiting for a different experiment. For a purchase decision today, the useful statement is that these trials do not provide a dependable performance promise.

The second study tested a named commercial preparation, not Wellness Nest's Turkesterone Advanced Formula. An ingredient appearing in both product names is not enough to transfer the result between them. Ingredient amounts, the complete formula, and the intervention itself would need to match before treating a trial as evidence for a particular jar.

## What does the shilajit strength study show?

**A purified shilajit preparation produced a limited signal for retaining strength after a laboratory fatigue protocol.** That is a more specific claim than saying shilajit builds muscle, prevents everyday tiredness, or outperforms turkesterone.

The [2019 shilajit trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30728074/) enrolled 63 recreationally active men and compared supplementation with placebo over eight weeks. Participants underwent strength testing and a fatiguing exercise protocol. The intervention was PrimaVie purified shilajit, not an unspecified resin and not the store product pictured in this article.

The abstract's reported significant difference in fatigue-related strength decline concerned the stronger half of participants at baseline. The higher-intake group had a smaller decline than the comparison groups in that subgroup. Preserving that qualification is essential: a subgroup result should not become a claim that everyone who takes shilajit will experience better workouts.

The measured endpoint was strength retention following a particular fatiguing task. It was not a head-to-head supplement contest. It did not establish that a shopper would gain more muscle, feel more energetic throughout the day, or obtain the same result from another preparation.

For background on the substance rather than this performance comparison, see the existing [guide to shilajit's history, properties, and processing](https://wellnessnest.co/blogs/news/what-is-shilajit-history-chemical-properties-processing). The trial cited here, not a general history article, is the basis for the narrow strength-retention discussion.
