## What separates shilajit from maca?

**Shilajit is a complex natural exudate; maca is an Andean plant whose root is used in supplements.** They are not interchangeable versions of the same ingredient. The [shilajit exercise paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30728074/) describes a mineral complex containing fulvic acids and other constituents. The [maca systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20691074/) identifies maca as *Lepidium meyenii*, a member of the mustard family.

That difference helps explain why comparing the front of two containers tells you little about comparative benefit. A resin's appearance is not a measure of exercise performance. A root's traditional use is not a demonstration that it will change how you feel. Neither is a substitute for checking the actual human evidence.

The useful comparison is narrower: what preparation was studied, in whom, against what control, and with what measured result? A favorable finding on one questionnaire cannot be ranked directly against a favorable finding on a laboratory exercise test. Calling one ingredient “stronger” skips the question of stronger for what.

## Shilajit: what did the strength study actually measure?

**The cited shilajit trial measured strength retention after a fatiguing exercise protocol, not ordinary afternoon energy.** It enrolled 63 recreationally active men and compared two amounts of a studied preparation with placebo over eight weeks. ## Maca root: what do the human studies support?

**Maca has limited human evidence concerning sexual desire, but the cited trial did not find a corresponding difference in testosterone.** A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study followed healthy men for 12 weeks. Participants received gelatinized maca or placebo, and researchers assessed self-perceived sexual desire alongside mood and hormone measurements. [Source: maca trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12472620/).

The abstract reports an improvement in desire from eight weeks, while testosterone and estradiol did not differ between the maca and placebo groups. That distinction is useful: a change in a reported experience is not automatically evidence of a hormone mechanism. It also does not establish a sexual-performance promise for an individual buyer.

The wider evidence check is less confident than a single positive headline. A [systematic review of four randomized trials](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20691074/) concluded that the available evidence was limited; the number of trials, their combined sample size and their methodological quality were insufficient for firm conclusions. Its search extended through April 2010, so it is a dated review, not a claim to summarize every subsequent paper.

The review included different populations and outcomes. That variety is a reason to read the actual study relevant to your goal, not permission to combine all positive findings into one broad promise. An outcome in adult men does not automatically establish a benefit for women, and a sexual-function questionnaire does not establish improved gym performance.

Maca may therefore be the more relevant ingredient to research if your question concerns the desire outcomes examined in those papers. “More relevant to research” is not the same as “recommended treatment,” and this comparison does not recommend either ingredient for a diagnosed condition.

## Is either better for energy or for women?

**The sources reviewed here do not establish that either ingredient is better for everyday energy or that one is the better choice for all women.** The shilajit trial used a fatiguing exercise test in men. The maca review discussed sexual-function studies, including one involving menopausal women, but still judged the overall evidence too limited for firm conclusions. [Sources: shilajit trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30728074/), [maca review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20691074/).

Start by writing down what “energy” means to you. Staying awake at a desk, recovering between training sessions and wanting more sexual interest are different goals. Evidence for one should not be quietly transferred to another. A comparison that promises all three from the same study is answering a broader question than the researchers tested.

For women considering either product, ask for research relevant to the specific goal and life stage rather than relying on a general “for women” label. If pregnancy, breastfeeding, a medical condition or medication use is part of your situation, take the exact product label to your clinician before deciding. This is a precautionary decision step, not a claim that these papers establish safety in those circumstances.

Choosing neither is a valid outcome. You do not need to buy the less uncertain of two products if neither addresses your actual question with evidence you find persuasive.
