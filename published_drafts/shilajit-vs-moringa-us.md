## What is shilajit, and what has actually been tested?

**Shilajit is a mineral-containing natural material, not a leafy vegetable.** A published exercise study describes it as an exudate containing fulvic acids and other constituents. That identity matters: comparing shilajit with moringa as though they were equivalent green supplements starts with the wrong premise. The [shilajit exercise paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30728074/) concerns a particular commercial ingredient, not every resin sold online.

Human studies provide specific questions to examine, not permission to promise a broad transformation. In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, purified shilajit was studied in healthy men aged 45–55 over 90 days. Researchers reported increases in total and free testosterone and DHEAS compared with placebo. Those are hormone measurements in a defined group—not proof of better daily energy, improved fertility or a result for every buyer. See the [original hormone-study abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26395129/).

A separate randomized study enrolled 63 recreationally active men over eight weeks. It tested strength before and after a demanding exercise protocol. The abstract reports a favorable fatigue-related strength finding in the stronger subgroup at the higher study intake. That narrow result should not become “shilajit builds muscle” or “everyone gets stronger.” The [exercise trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30728074/) also identifies the tested ingredient as PrimaVie, not ShilaSource resin.

For a reader deciding whether shilajit is better than moringa, the useful distinction is **closer research relevance**, not established superiority. Someone interested in exercise research may find shilajit the more relevant ingredient to investigate. That is still several steps away from proving that the retail jar in their cart produces the trial's result.

## What does the moringa evidence say?

**Moringa leaf research asks different questions from the shilajit trials above.** A randomized placebo-controlled study tested Moringa oleifera leaf capsules in 32 people with type 2 diabetes over four weeks. It found no significant between-group difference in fasting plasma glucose or HbA1C. The authors also reported no adverse effects during that short study. Read the [moringa leaf trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29317895/).

The result has two boundaries. First, it does not support selling moringa as a dependable blood-sugar solution. Second, a short trial with a small group does not establish long-term safety for every preparation or person. “No adverse effects reported here” and “safe for everyone” are very different statements.

Other nutritional research is also population-specific. A trial compared moringa leaf powder with nutritional counseling among adults receiving antiretroviral therapy in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. The moringa group showed greater increases in BMI and albumin after six months. That [nutrition study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28830411/) does not establish a benefit for a well-nourished US shopper, nor does it compare moringa with shilajit.

If your interest is a leaf-based ingredient rather than resin, start by identifying the exact moringa product: leaf powder, leaf capsules or a named extract.
