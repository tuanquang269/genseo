**NMN body odor is not established by the sources reviewed here as a direct NMN effect.** For readers in the US, a new smell after starting an NMN + TMG supplement deserves a full-label review, not an automatic diagnosis. ## Does NMN cause body odor?

**The evidence reviewed here does not establish that NMN itself causes body odor.** That is different from promising it cannot happen. The useful question is what changed, which ingredients you took, and whether the odor comes from sweat, breath, urine, or the bottle itself.

A combination product creates an attribution problem: starting several ingredients together cannot tell you which ingredient, if any, explains a symptom. A new smell also does not demonstrate that the product is working. Do not treat it as proof of improved metabolism, a successful “detox,” or a reason to increase your intake.

## What does the TMG odor evidence actually say?

**TMG is another name for betaine, and odor complaints appear in prescription betaine labeling.** The [DailyMed Cystadane prescribing information](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=31a684d0-cd98-4e46-b43a-ccbd17c41559) identifies its ingredient as anhydrous betaine, or trimethylglycine. It lists “Caused Odor” in a retrospective physician survey and “skin odor abnormalities” among postapproval reports.

The limitations are central to interpreting those entries. The label says the survey depended on physician recall rather than systematic, controlled collection. Voluntary reports cannot reliably establish how often an event occurs or whether the medicine caused it. Turning those observations into an NMN supplement side-effect rate would misrepresent the evidence.

Cystadane is a prescription treatment for certain forms of homocystinuria. Its patient population and treatment context are not interchangeable with an adult buying a combination supplement. The reports make TMG worth mentioning during a clinician or pharmacist review; they do not identify the culprit in your bottle.

## Is fishy odor the same as trimethylaminuria?

**A fishy smell alone does not establish trimethylaminuria.** [NIH MedlinePlus Genetics](https://medlineplus.gov/genetics/condition/trimethylaminuria/) describes the condition as a buildup of trimethylamine, a compound with an odor resembling spoiled fish. The odor can appear in perspiration, urine, and exhaled air.

The FMO3 gene provides instructions for an enzyme that normally converts trimethylamine into an odorless product. Certain genetic changes disrupt that process. That explains the condition; it does not show that everyone noticing odor after a supplement has an FMO3 problem.

Keep **trimethylamine** separate from **trimethylglycine**, the name used for betaine. Similar names are not interchangeable diagnoses. Nor should a discussion of trimethylamine N-oxide, often shortened to TMAO, be used as proof that an NMN + TMG capsule caused a fishy smell. The evidence cited here does not establish that chain for this product.

If an odor persists, explain the pattern to a clinician instead of ordering genetic tests or removing whole food groups on your own. Bring the actual container and a list of other products you use.

## How should you track a new smell?

**Record a clear timeline before changing several things at once.** The goal is to make a healthcare conversation useful, not to run a home challenge test. Avoid restarting a suspected product just to see whether an unpleasant reaction returns.

## When should urine odor prompt medical care?

**Look at associated symptoms, not just the strength of the smell.** [NHS urine-odor guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/smelly-urine/) lists coffee, asparagus, insufficient fluid, some medicines, and vitamin B6 supplements among possible causes. It also notes that painful or frequent urination and cloudy urine can accompany a urinary tract infection.

In the US, contact your healthcare provider for burning, increased urgency, or persistent unexplained changes. Seek urgent medical assessment for blood in urine, fever or chills, pain in the lower abdomen or back beneath the ribs, or feeling significantly unwell. Confusion warrants urgent attention. Do not wait for a supplement experiment to resolve those symptoms.

For odor alone, adequate fluid intake may help if you have been drinking too little. Follow any clinician-prescribed fluid restriction rather than forcing extra water. If the smell continues, get advice even if the timing seems to implicate a supplement.
