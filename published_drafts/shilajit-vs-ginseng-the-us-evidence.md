## Shilajit: what did the exercise trial show?

**The shilajit trial offers a limited signal about resistance to exercise-related strength loss—not proof of better everyday energy.** Researchers studied 63 healthy, recreationally active men over eight weeks, comparing two amounts of purified shilajit with placebo. Their average age was about 21 years.

The higher-amount group performed better than the lower-amount group on fatigue-related isometric strength loss across the full sample. A favorable comparison with placebo appeared in the subgroup of stronger participants. The trial did not demonstrate an improvement in unfatigued strength or concentric peak torque. These details are reported in the [full published paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6364418/).

Why does the subgroup matter? A result among selected participants is narrower than a result across everyone enrolled. It should not become a headline saying shilajit improves energy in all adults. Nor does a laboratory exercise protocol establish how someone will feel during a workday.

The study was funded by Natreon, the supplier of the tested preparation. Funding does not automatically invalidate a result, but it belongs in the reader's assessment alongside the small sample and restricted population. The study also does not establish long-term safety.

## Ginseng: what did the fatigue trial show?

**Ginseng has been studied using fatigue questionnaires, but the findings do not support a blanket promise of feeling more energetic.** A randomized, double-blind trial enrolled 90 adults with unexplained persistent fatigue and compared Panax ginseng extract with placebo for four weeks.

The overall numeric fatigue score did not show a statistically significant difference between groups. Mental-fatigue scores improved relative to placebo at both tested amounts, while a separate visual-analogue fatigue measure improved significantly only at the higher amount. One participant stopped after a rash and itching. The [full trial report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3629193/) supplies those results.

That pattern is mixed, not uniformly positive. If a summary mentions only the improved score and leaves out the nonsignificant overall score, it gives a buyer an incomplete picture. The trial was also short and relatively small; it does not establish that all ginseng preparations behave alike.

The broader [NCCIH evidence summary](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/asian-ginseng) says Asian ginseng may have a small beneficial effect on general fatigue unrelated to illness, while study results vary. That is a more defensible expectation than calling ginseng an instant energy solution.

If perceived fatigue is your research priority, this literature addresses your question more directly than a leg-extension strength-loss test. More directly relevant does not mean guaranteed to work, safer for you, or proven superior to shilajit. The studies reviewed here did not compare the two head-to-head.
