## What did the NMN human trial measure?

**The cited NMN trial studied muscle insulin sensitivity in postmenopausal women with prediabetes who had overweight or obesity.** It did not test NMN against C15:0, and it did not measure whether participants lived longer.

## What did the C15:0 human studies measure?

**C15:0 has human randomized-trial evidence, but the studies reviewed here do not establish a general longevity benefit.** They address supplementation, circulating levels, and metabolic outcomes in defined groups.

### The young-adult supplementation trial

A [randomized C15:0 study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39069269/) included 30 young adults with overweight or obesity and ran for 12 weeks. Its primary objective was to investigate changes in plasma C15:0 levels. The supplemented group had a greater increase in circulating C15:0 than the placebo group, and the abstract reported no significant adverse events.

That primary finding demonstrates a change in the measured blood level under the study conditions. It is not the same finding as better daily functioning, disease prevention, or extended lifespan. A blood marker can be a useful research endpoint without being a complete answer to a consumer's health goal.

The paper also reported potentially relevant changes in liver enzymes and hemoglobin among participants who reached a specified post-treatment C15:0 level. Those were comparisons based on achieved blood levels. They should not be presented as a simple promise that everyone randomized to the supplement obtained those benefits.

The authors called for further study. The [full paper's funding statement](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12612588/) reports support from Seraphina Therapeutics, including provision of supplements and matching placebo. Industry support does not automatically invalidate a study, but it belongs in an honest reading alongside the design, outcomes, and limitations.

### The diet-plus-supplement trial

The [TANGO randomized trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38035997/) enrolled 88 Chinese women with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease. Over 12 weeks, it compared an adapted Mediterranean-style diet with C15:0, the diet without C15:0, and a habitual-diet control group.

Both intervention-diet groups improved several outcomes compared with the control group. The abstract also reported further LDL-cholesterol reduction with C15:0 supplementation. These results concern a dietary intervention and a specific clinical population, not a stand-alone demonstration that a capsule improves everyone's health.

The practical reading error would be to attribute all improvements in the diet-plus-supplement group to C15:0. Because the study included a diet-without-C15:0 group, readers should look for the incremental supplement finding rather than assigning the entire dietary result to the supplement.

## How should you check a longevity claim?

**Ask what changed, in whom, and against which comparator.** Those questions help distinguish a documented result from an attractive interpretation.

Start with the endpoint. “Blood levels increased” describes exposure to an ingredient. “A metabolic measurement changed” describes a physiological result. “People lived longer” would describe a different outcome altogether. The trials cited here should be described using the outcomes they actually reported.

Next, identify the population. Do not silently remove “postmenopausal women with prediabetes” or “young adults with overweight or obesity” from a study summary. Those descriptions are not incidental details: they define who supplied the evidence.

Then examine the comparison. The C15:0 diet trial illustrates why the control matters. If the food pattern changed along with the supplement, the reader needs the diet-only comparison to understand what the supplement added. In the supplementation trial, grouping people by their achieved blood levels raises a different interpretive question from the original randomized comparison.

Finally, ask whether the retail formula matches the research intervention. Ingredient identity alone is not enough to declare a finished product clinically validated. Keep the study citation beside the study statement, not beside a sales promise that the paper did not test.
