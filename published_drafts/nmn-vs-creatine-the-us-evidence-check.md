For buyers in the US, creatine is the better-supported choice for high-intensity exercise and training adaptations; NMN addresses a different research question around NAD metabolism. Human NMN findings do not establish it as a substitute for creatine. Choose by the outcome you want, not by the shared promise of “energy.”

## What does NMN research actually show?

**NMN is a precursor involved in NAD biosynthesis, but a change in a biomarker is not automatically a change in strength, muscle size or lifespan.** The relevant question is what researchers measured in people, rather than what the pathway might theoretically do.

The [NMN trial in postmenopausal women with prediabetes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33888596/) reported improved muscle insulin sensitivity and insulin signaling. Participants were overweight or obese, and the trial lasted ten weeks. Those details matter: the result concerns a particular metabolic outcome in a particular population. It is not evidence that NMN builds muscle like a resistance-training supplement or that every healthy adult should expect the same response.

A separate [trial in healthy older men](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35927255/) found increased blood NAD and related metabolites. The authors also reported nominally significant improvements in gait speed and a left-hand grip test, while explicitly calling for validation in larger studies. Body composition did not change significantly. Reporting only the favorable grip finding would leave out both its uncertainty and the negative body-composition result.

That makes NMN worth discussing as a research topic, not a guaranteed upgrade for lifting. If an advertisement turns “raises NAD” into “reverses aging” or “replaces creatine,” ask which human outcome bridges that gap. Neither of these trials supplies that conclusion.

For the distinction between an individual ingredient and the stocked blend, see the existing [NMN alone versus NMN + TMG guide](https://wellnessnest.co/blogs/news/nmn-alone-vs-nmn-tmg-which-fits-your-goal). The narrower question here is whether NMN evidence supports choosing it instead of creatine for a training goal.

## What does creatine research support?

**Creatine has a more established evidence base for high-intensity exercise and training adaptations.** The [ISSN position stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28615996/) describes increased intramuscular creatine concentrations and improvements in high-intensity exercise performance across the literature it reviews.

That is a closer match to a shopper asking about repeated hard efforts than an NMN study measuring a blood biomarker. It also explains why “both support energy” is an inadequate buying comparison: the relevant endpoint is the activity or function being tested, not a broad marketing word.

The position stand is a synthesis of research, not a clinical trial of every retail creatine product. Ingredient-level evidence cannot certify an unfamiliar gummy, chew, capsule or flavored blend. Before buying, check the actual ingredient identity, labeled serving information and any independently verifiable testing documentation. A recognizable ingredient name is not a substitute for knowing what the finished product contains.

A useful training question is specific: “Does this evidence concern the type of exercise I do?” A less useful question is whether one supplement sounds more modern. For a strength-oriented routine, start with the evidence that addresses training rather than assuming longevity positioning makes a product a better fit.

## Does the NMN running study change the verdict?

**The running study adds an exercise-related finding, but it does not establish that NMN outperforms creatine.** Its design and measured outcomes are more informative than the word “aerobic” in a headline.

The [randomized NMN running trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34238308/) included 48 recreationally trained runners over six weeks. It reported improvements in selected ventilatory-threshold outcomes with supplementation and training. However, the abstract reported no difference in maximum oxygen uptake, oxygen pulse or peak power after treatment compared with baseline in any group.

The practical distinction is between a selected threshold measurement and a blanket promise of better athletic performance. The study did not compare NMN with creatine, did not test the AgeAura combination sold here, and did not demonstrate a general muscle-building benefit. Those limitations belong beside the positive result, not hidden after a sales recommendation.

A runner can reasonably be interested in this paper while still recognizing that it answers a different question from a lifter choosing a supplement for high-intensity training. Different outcomes can both be scientifically interesting without making the products interchangeable.

## How should you choose without buying an unnecessary stack?

**Write down the desired outcome before comparing products.** “I want better results” is too broad to distinguish these research programs; “I am choosing around high-intensity training” is more useful.

Use this decision process:

1. **Name the goal.** Decide whether the question concerns exercise performance, a clinician-discussed metabolic issue, or curiosity about NAD research. Avoid treating all three as the same purchase.
2. **Match the evidence.** Look for the outcome, population and formulation in the paper. A metabolic study in a selected population is not an all-purpose recommendation.
3. **Separate the ingredient from the bottle.** Check whether the study investigated a single ingredient while the retail product combines ingredients. Do not transfer the trial result unchanged.
4. **Ask what would justify the cost.** A label's “energy” wording does not define a measurable personal benefit. Decide what information you still need before placing an order.
5. **Keep the option of buying neither.** Uncertainty is a legitimate reason to wait, especially when the proposed purchase relies on extrapolation rather than a relevant measured outcome.

The studies cited here do not evaluate an NMN-plus-creatine stack. Therefore, they cannot establish that buying both is better, or establish the combination's safety for your circumstances. Different research pathways are a reason to ask a better question, not proof of synergy.

For personalized decisions, bring the complete labels and your medication list to a qualified clinician. This article is not a dosing plan. If your concern is persistent fatigue or a change in physical function, seek an assessment rather than assuming a supplement comparison explains the cause.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is NMN better than creatine for building muscle?

The evidence reviewed here does not support that claim. The [creatine position stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28615996/) is more directly relevant to high-intensity exercise and training adaptations. The [older-men NMN trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35927255/) reported no significant body-composition effect. That is a reason not to market NMN as an established muscle-building replacement.

### Does higher NAD mean more strength?

Not automatically. Blood NAD is a biomarker, while strength is a functional outcome. The [older-men NMN study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35927255/) reported both a NAD increase and limited functional signals, with the authors calling for larger studies to validate those signals. The biomarker alone does not settle the performance question.

### Can I take NMN and creatine together?

The cited research does not establish a combined regimen or its benefit. Do not interpret the absence of a combination test as proof of safety or synergy. Ask a qualified clinician to review the specific products, health conditions and medications before deciding whether combining them is appropriate.
