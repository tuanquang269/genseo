## What does NMN human evidence actually show?

**NMN has a randomized human study reporting improved muscle insulin sensitivity in a specific population, not proof of longer life.** NMN stands for nicotinamide mononucleotide and is involved in NAD+ biosynthesis. That biological role explains the research question; it does not establish that every person benefits from taking a supplement.

In a randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind trial lasting 10 weeks, researchers studied postmenopausal women with prediabetes who were overweight or obese. Insulin-stimulated glucose disposal and muscle insulin signaling increased with NMN supplementation but did not change after placebo treatment. Those details come from the [Yoshino and colleagues primary study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33888596/).

Several boundaries matter when reading that result. Participants were not an unrestricted sample of healthy adults. The outcome was a measured metabolic response, not a lifespan endpoint. The intervention was NMN, not an automatic validation of every multi-ingredient supplement containing NMN. A shopper should retain all those qualifiers when repeating the finding.

For someone comparing supplements because of a glucose concern, the study is a reason to ask a qualified clinician about the evidence—not to replace testing, prescribed treatment or follow-up. For someone seeking general longevity, it is an interesting piece of research without an answer to the larger question of how long they will live.

The practical buying question is therefore specific: does the research outcome match the outcome you care about? If the advertisement promises something the study did not measure, the advertisement has gone further than the evidence.

## What does AKG human evidence actually show?

**AKG, short for alpha-ketoglutarate, has human biological-age research, but the design of each paper determines what it can establish.** A retrospective report, a trial protocol and a recruitment report are different kinds of evidence. Treating them as interchangeable makes the evidence look stronger than it is.

An often-discussed [2021 retrospective study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34847066/) examined DNA methylation age in 42 people taking an alpha-ketoglutarate-based formulation with vitamins. The paper reported a lower biological-age estimate after supplementation. It was not a randomized placebo-controlled comparison of AKG against NMN.

The formulation matters as much as the headline. A result from AKG combined with vitamins cannot isolate the contribution of AKG alone. Nor can a before-and-after report tell a shopper what would have happened to the same participants without the intervention. Its findings can motivate further research without settling a buying decision.

The [ABLE study protocol](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37217632/) describes a double-blind, placebo-controlled randomized trial of sustained-release calcium alpha-ketoglutarate. Its planned outcomes include DNA methylation age and measures of physical function. A protocol tells readers what investigators intend to test; it does not supply the treatment results.

A later [ABLE recruitment evaluation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40819772/) reports the feasibility of enrolling biologically older, generally healthy middle-aged adults. Recruitment success is useful information for conducting trials. It is not evidence that the intervention slowed aging. Readers should check the abstract's actual results rather than infer efficacy from the word “trial” in a title.

A [2026 observational cohort paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42166733/) also found an association between a delayed-release calcium-AKG-plus-vitamin supplement and a biological-age measure. The authors identify healthy-user recruitment bias and the need for controlled trials. Association remains a different claim from a treatment effect, even when a dataset is larger than an earlier report.

## How should you read biological-age claims?

**Read a biological-age claim as a result from a particular measurement, not a promise about remaining lifespan.** The AKG reports cited above use DNA methylation-based age estimates. Their reported outcomes are not observations of participants living additional years.

Use this short source-checking sequence before accepting a headline:

1. **Identify the design.** Was it randomized, observational, retrospective, or only a protocol? Write that word beside the headline.
2. **Identify the intervention.** Was it the ingredient alone, a particular salt or release format, or a combination with vitamins?
3. **Identify the measurement.** Did investigators measure a clock estimate, metabolic function, physical performance, or an actual health outcome?
4. **Identify the comparison.** Was there a placebo group? Were groups followed over time, or were users compared observationally?
5. **Identify the limit.** Ask what the design cannot establish before applying the result to your own life.

For example, “participants had a lower age estimate after taking a combination” is not equivalent to “AKG makes people live longer.” Likewise, “NMN improved a muscle measurement in this population” is not equivalent to “this bottle improves every buyer's metabolism.” Keeping the wording narrow is more informative than choosing a dramatic winner.
