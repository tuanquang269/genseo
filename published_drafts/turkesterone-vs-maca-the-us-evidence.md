## How should you judge strength and energy claims?

**Start with the endpoint, not the headline.** Write down whether you want a measurable training improvement, a change in perceived fatigue or help understanding a persistent symptom. Then ask whether the research actually studied that outcome.

For example, a training log might record the exercise, resistance used and how the session felt. That is a practical way to define your question; it is not a clinical test proving that a supplement caused any improvement. Without a suitable comparison, an individual change can be difficult to interpret.

Use this reading checklist before treating a study as a reason to buy:

- **Population:** Were the participants humans, and were their circumstances relevant to yours?
- **Preparation:** Was the tested material the finished product you are considering, or something different?
- **Comparison:** Did the supplement outperform a control, or did participants merely improve from their starting point?
- **Outcome:** Did researchers measure the benefit named in the advertisement?
- **Duration:** Was the observation long enough to address the promise being made?
- **Limitations:** Did the authors describe uncertainty that the sales summary left out?

These questions explain why the turkesterone and maca evidence cannot be collapsed into a single winner badge. A study can be worth reading while still being insufficient grounds for a purchase. If ongoing fatigue is the reason you are shopping, seek individualized medical advice rather than treating a comparison article as a diagnosis.
