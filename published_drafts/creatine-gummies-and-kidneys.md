Creatine gummies and kidneys raise two different questions: actual kidney function and the blood marker creatinine. Research in healthy adults is reassuring for creatine monohydrate, but gummies are not automatically proven safe by those studies. In the US, tell your clinician about supplementation before bloodwork; existing kidney disease needs individual advice, not reassurance from an athlete study.

## Does creatine raise creatinine without damaging kidneys?

**It can. Creatinine is a breakdown product of creatine, so a higher reading does not automatically prove kidney damage.** The opposite shortcut is also wrong: taking creatine does not make an abnormal result safe to ignore.

A [review of creatine and kidney-function assessment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10054094/) explains the distinction. Creatine contributes to the body's creatinine production. Blood creatinine is useful for assessing kidney function, but its concentration reflects more than filtration alone. Supplement use can therefore complicate interpretation.

The lab may also report estimated glomerular filtration rate, or eGFR. When that estimate is calculated from creatinine, a supplement-related change in creatinine can affect the estimate. That is not the same as directly measuring how well the kidneys filter blood.

Keep the vocabulary separate:

- **Creatine:** the supplement ingredient being discussed.
- **Creatinine:** the breakdown product measured in blood and urine.
- **Creatinine-based eGFR:** an estimate that uses that blood marker, not an independent second measurement.

The useful question is not “Can I explain away this number?” It is “Does this result fit my history, earlier tests, supplement use, and other findings?” Bring that question to the clinician who ordered the test.

## What did long-term studies actually find?

**Long-term athlete studies did not find the renal-marker deterioration often claimed in blanket warnings about creatine.** Their populations and methods still limit what those findings can promise an individual buyer.

In [Poortmans and Francaux's study](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/10449011), creatine users with supplementation histories of 10 months to five years were compared with a control group. The investigators reported no statistical differences in the measured creatinine, urea, and albumin outcomes or their clearances. This was a comparison of healthy athletes, not a trial proving safety for people with established kidney disease.

A separate [Kreider and colleagues study](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12701816) followed 98 college football players over a 21-month period. It used an open-label design, with creatine and non-creatine supplement groups. The researchers found no clinically significant adverse differences in the assessed health markers, including renal assessments. Open-label athlete research is useful evidence, but it is not the same as a blinded trial in every age group and medical condition.

The [International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5469049/) places these findings within the wider creatine literature. Its renal-safety discussion is reassuring. It does not turn every commercial gummy formulation into a product with its own kidney-safety trial.

| Evidence or test | What it helps establish | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term studies in healthy athletes | Whether measured markers worsened in the studied groups | Safety for someone with existing kidney disease |
| Blood creatinine | A marker clinicians use to assess filtration | Whether a change came only from supplementation |
| Creatinine-based eGFR | An estimate calculated using creatinine | An independent confirmation unaffected by that marker |
| Assessment using other markers or measured filtration | Additional context when creatinine is difficult to interpret | A reason to skip individualized clinical review |

These distinctions matter more than a simple “safe” badge. The evidence supports a narrower statement: studied creatine protocols have reassuring renal findings in healthy participants. It does not support “creatine cannot harm anyone” or “every gummy has been tested.”

## Who should ask a clinician before taking creatine gummies?

**People with known kidney disease, reduced kidney function, or unexplained abnormal kidney tests should get individualized advice before starting.** The kidney review notes that evidence in pre-existing kidney disease remains insufficient and advises particular caution when filtration is very low.

If you already receive kidney care, take the exact product label to that team. Do not use an athlete's routine or a supplement retailer's reassurance as a substitute for your own treatment plan. If you take medicines or several supplements, bring the complete list rather than asking about creatine in isolation.

For a healthy adult without known kidney problems, the research is more reassuring. Even then, an abnormal result deserves follow-up. This article cannot decide whether a particular result is a harmless marker change or evidence of a medical problem.

Do not stop prescribed medication, postpone a recommended test, or restart a supplement against your clinician's advice because of something read here. The practical goal is better information at the appointment, not self-diagnosis.

## How should you prepare for bloodwork in the US?

**Tell the ordering clinician what you take before the sample is collected, and follow their preparation instructions.** There is no universal supplement pause that this article can prescribe for every person and test.

Use this appointment checklist:

1. **Bring the actual package or clear label photographs.** Include every ingredient, not just the creatine name on the front.
2. **Record your routine.** Write down when you started, how much you actually take, and whether that changed recently.
3. **List other products and medicines.** Include powders, pre-workouts, and anything used only on training days.
4. **Ask how the result will be interpreted.** Mention prior abnormal tests and ask whether earlier results are available for comparison.
5. **Get specific follow-up instructions.** If the result is abnormal, ask what should happen next rather than changing your routine to chase a better number.

When creatinine is difficult to interpret, the [kidney-function review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10054094/) discusses alternatives such as cystatin C and measured filtration. Ask whether an additional assessment would be useful in your case. Do not assume that every supplement user needs every test, or that a different marker is automatically conclusive.
