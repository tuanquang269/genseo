For people in the US asking whether shilajit increases sperm count, one small clinical trial found improved semen measures after 90 days of processed shilajit in men with low sperm counts. That result does not prove higher pregnancy or live-birth rates, does not establish that every shilajit resin has the same composition, and does not replace a fertility evaluation.

## What did the shilajit fertility trial find?

The study most often cited for shilajit and male fertility was a clinical trial published in *Andrologia* in 2010. Researchers initially assessed 60 infertile men and enrolled 35 men whose total sperm count was below the study's threshold for oligospermia. Twenty-eight participants completed the 90-day treatment period. The abstract reports improvements from baseline in sperm-related measures, including total sperm count, sperm motility, and normal sperm count.

The source is **Biswas TK et al., “Clinical evaluation of spermatogenic activity of processed Shilajit in oligospermia,” *Andrologia* 42(1):48–56 (2010)**. The PubMed record identifies the paper as a clinical trial and gives the DOI [10.1111/j.1439-0272.2009.00956.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1439-0272.2009.00956.x). Its abstract is available through [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/20078516).

The study also measured hormones and a marker of oxidative stress. Those findings can support a hypothesis for future research; they cannot establish that shilajit improves conception odds. The study did not show a pregnancy-rate, live-birth, or time-to-pregnancy benefit.

## Does shilajit increase sperm count?

It may affect semen measures in some men, but current evidence is too limited to say that shilajit reliably increases sperm count or improves fertility. The single trial had a small completing group, compared results with participants' own baselines, and studied a processed preparation under specific conditions. A baseline comparison is not the same as a large, well-controlled trial against placebo.

A useful reading of the result is: **shilajit deserves further study as a research question, not a proven fertility treatment**. A semen analysis, medical history, medication review, and clinician assessment answer more important questions than a supplement claim alone. Male-factor issues can contribute to difficulty conceiving, so both partners may need assessment.

### What the trial cannot show

- It cannot show that an over-the-counter resin has the same identity, purity, or processing as the study material.
- It cannot show that semen changes lead to pregnancy or a live birth.
- It cannot show that the result applies to men with normal sperm counts, different diagnoses, or different health conditions.
- It cannot establish a fertility regimen for a couple.
- It cannot establish long-term safety for every person or product.

The study is still useful because it gives readers a concrete evidence boundary: a small 90-day clinical trial, not a definitive answer for all people trying to conceive.

## How long did the shilajit trial last?

The treatment period lasted 90 days. The researchers evaluated semen and blood measures before treatment and at the end of that period. That timing is relevant to the paper, but it is not a promise that 90 days is the right timeframe for an individual or that repeating the study schedule is appropriate outside clinical care.

Sperm production and fertility involve multiple factors, including age, health conditions, medications, heat exposure, tobacco or cannabis use, alcohol, sleep, and reproductive timing. A supplement cannot be used to infer which factor is affecting a couple's fertility.

The trial also had a completion gap: 35 men enrolled, while 28 completed treatment. That attrition matters when interpreting the findings. The abstract reports statistically significant within-study changes, but the design and size leave substantial uncertainty about how reproducible the effect would be.

## Should partners trying to conceive take shilajit?

A partner who is pregnant, nursing, or trying to become pregnant should not start a shilajit or Ayurvedic supplement without advice from a qualified healthcare professional. The same caution applies to anyone taking prescription medicines, managing a chronic condition, or considering a supplement after an abnormal fertility test.

The [NCCIH overview of Ayurvedic medicine](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ayurvedic-medicine-in-depth) warns that some Ayurvedic preparations may contain toxic metals and advises people who are pregnant or nursing to consult a healthcare provider. ## When should you see a fertility specialist?

Seek care sooner for a known or suspected condition that can affect fertility, sexual dysfunction, a history of testicular injury or surgery, recurrent pregnancy loss, or an abnormal semen analysis. Otherwise, the appropriate timing depends on both partners' ages and medical history.

The [American Society for Reproductive Medicine committee opinion](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/practice-committee-documents/fertility-evaluation-of-infertile-women-a-committee-opinion-2021/) says evaluation may begin after 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse when the female partner is younger than 35, after 6 months when the female partner is 35 or older, and more immediately when the female partner is over 40 or a relevant condition is present. It also states that, when a male partner contributes to the pregnancy, evaluation of both partners should begin at the same time and a semen analysis is part of the initial assessment.

A fertility clinician can decide whether to repeat semen testing, review medications, examine hormone or genetic factors, or refer to a reproductive urologist. Bring the full supplement label and any certificate-of-analysis information to that visit. Stop and ask for medical advice if a product causes a reaction or if symptoms develop.
