NMN for fertility remains experimental: the human research reviewed here does not establish that it improves live birth or makes conception safer. For people in the US, a small fertility conference abstract is not a treatment recommendation. Animal findings are interesting, but they cannot establish a human fertility dose or pregnancy safety.

## What human NMN fertility research actually exists?

**A small human conference abstract reports preliminary fertility observations, not reliable proof that NMN increases the chance of taking home a baby.** Its design and reporting limitations matter more than an appealing pregnancy percentage.

A [2025 Human Reproduction conference abstract](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/40/Supplement_1/deaf097.217/8171099) describes 50 women with diminished ovarian reserve, divided into groups receiving ovarian stimulation with or without NMN. It describes the study as retrospective, while its table also uses randomization language without explaining an allocation method. That inconsistency means readers should not confidently label it a randomized controlled trial.

The abstract reports 12 pregnancies among 25 participants in the NMN group, compared with six among 25 controls. Those are small groups, and the abstract does not provide live-birth counts. Pregnancy, ongoing pregnancy and live birth are different outcomes; substituting one for another changes what a result means. The abstract also reports follicle and endometrial measurements, but those observations do not prove that NMN caused the pregnancy difference.

This is a conference abstract rather than a detailed full study report. Its NMN regimen is not sufficiently specified to support a practical fertility dosing recommendation. It is useful as a reason to investigate the question further, not as a shopping prescription. No completed human randomized trial establishing a fertility benefit was identified in the sources reviewed for this article; that is a description of this evidence review, not a claim that every unpublished project has been checked.

## What did animal studies measure instead?

**Mouse egg and birth-outcome experiments provide a biological research lead, not a human treatment protocol.** Species, experimental setting and the outcome measured must stay attached to every finding.

In [Bertoldo and colleagues’ 2020 Cell Reports study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7063679/), researchers studied reproductive aging in mice. NMN administration improved some measures of oocyte quality and reproductive outcomes in aged female mice. Separate experiments also added NMN directly to embryo culture medium. Drinking an oral supplement and treating cells in a laboratory dish are not interchangeable interventions.

The same paper did not show a simple rule that a higher amount produced a better reproductive result. That matters for readers tempted to translate an animal experiment into a larger capsule intake. A mouse experiment cannot supply a recommended amount for a person trying to conceive.

A [2025 systematic review and human-oocyte analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12831783/) identified seven preclinical intervention studies. It also analyzed gene-expression data from human oocytes. That human component was descriptive research, not a demonstration that giving women NMN improves their eggs. The authors called for standardized protocols and clinical trials.

## How should you compare NMN and fertility claims?

**Compare the population, intervention and endpoint before comparing the headline.** A result can be genuine while the claim built around it is much broader than the experiment supports.

| Evidence source | What was examined | What it cannot establish |
|---|---|---|
| Human conference abstract | Small groups receiving fertility stimulation with or without NMN | A confirmed causal effect or improved live birth |
| Aged-female mouse study | Oocytes, embryo development and reproductive outcomes in mice | Human conception benefit or a human fertility dose |
| Human-oocyte gene-expression analysis | Biological pathways in collected human oocytes | Benefit from an oral NMN supplement |
| Male mouse experiments | Sperm-related measures in particular experimental models | Improved sperm or conception outcomes in men |

For a claim about **NMN ovarian reserve**, ask whether the study measured a laboratory marker, retrieved eggs, pregnancy or live birth. Do not let “egg quality” stand in for all of them. For a claim about **NMN and sperm**, ask whether the participants were men or laboratory animals, and whether the experiment used a specific injury or metabolic model.

The practical reading habit is simple: underline the species and the endpoint in the source. If either disappears from a social post, return to the paper before acting. A supplement label cannot fill a missing clinical outcome.

## Does NMN improve sperm in men?

**The animal papers reviewed here do not establish improved sperm or fertility in men.** They also do not tell a consistent, context-free story that NMN always helps reproduction.

A [male-mouse study involving obesity](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12700873/) reported improved spermatogenesis-related outcomes with NMN. That is a finding in an experimental metabolic setting, not a clinical trial in men attempting conception.

A different [mouse study involving maternal obesity and male offspring](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6589912/) reported adverse sperm-related findings under its NMN treatment conditions. The experimental design and administration differed from the positive study. Neither result should be rewritten as a prediction for an individual person, but the contrast makes “NMN boosts sperm” an unjustified blanket promise.

If sperm testing or fertility treatment is already planned, take the exact product label to the clinician handling that evaluation. Do not replace a scheduled assessment with a supplement experiment based on a rodent headline.
