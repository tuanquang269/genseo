## 2. Check the potency promise through expiry

**Ask whether the stated viable quantity is guaranteed through the end of shelf life.** That is the standard recommended in [ISAPP's consumer guidance](https://isappscience.org/resource/how-to-choose-a-probiotic/). Do not silently reinterpret an “at manufacture” statement as an expiry guarantee.

When comparing CFU statements, copy the quantity, the serving it refers to, and the timing qualifier together. If a seller gives only a number, ask: “Does this apply through the printed expiry date when stored as directed?” Keep the response with your purchase record.

Also inspect the actual use-by date. An image on a product page does not establish the date on the pack being shipped. Before placing a larger order, ask what remaining shelf life the seller can confirm. This is a practical purchasing question, not a demand that every product carry identical wording.

An unclear expiry promise belongs in the “unconfirmed” column. It is not a reason to announce that laboratory testing has found dead organisms when no such test was performed.

## 3. Read storage directions before ordering

**Follow the product's stated temperature and storage requirements.** ISAPP notes that refrigeration needs depend on the formulation. “Refrigerated” and “shelf-stable” are not competing authenticity certificates.

Read the storage line before choosing where to keep the pack. If the label and website disagree, request a written clarification rather than choosing whichever instruction is easier. Ask whether the directions change after opening, and retain the answer.

For online orders in the US, a useful shipping question is whether the seller's handling matches its own storage requirements. Do not infer a delivery-temperature guarantee from a general shipping estimate. If a shipment arrives in a condition that concerns you, photograph the pack and contact the seller before using it.

## 4. Ask what “third-party tested” actually covers

**Request the document behind the testing claim.** As a buyer's checklist, ask for the laboratory name, report date, product identification, batch identification where applicable, and the tests performed. Then compare those details with the product offered.

Do not turn a general testing badge into an assumption that every question has been answered. Ask separately about identity, stated viable quantity, and any contaminant testing the seller advertises. If a report covers an ingredient rather than the finished product, record that distinction.

If a certification mark appears, ask for its current listing with the named organization. A logo in an advertisement is a starting point for verification, not the end of it. A seller declining to provide a public report is not conclusive evidence of counterfeiting; you can still decide the available information is insufficient for your purchase.

## 5. Separate realistic claims from drug promises

**Do not use “FDA-approved supplement” as a quality shortcut.** The [FDA's dietary supplement questions and answers](https://www.fda.gov/food/information-consumers-using-dietary-supplements/questions-and-answers-dietary-supplements) explains that supplements are not approved by the agency before marketing. Claims to treat, prevent, or cure a specific disease fall under drug regulation.

A product's genuine origin would not make an unsupported medical promise reliable. Ask for evidence matching the exact claim rather than accepting a review screenshot as the answer. Likewise, a disclaimer does not answer a question about strain identity or batch testing.

For symptoms or a suspected infection, seek clinical advice rather than replacing evaluation with a supplement purchase. Use the label checks to identify missing information, then request documentation from the seller. A package photograph cannot verify contents. Keep authenticity, quality specifications, and evidence of benefit as separate questions; none should be assumed from the others.

ISAPP recommends a viable quantity guaranteed through the end of shelf life. If the label only describes the quantity at manufacture, ask whether a separate expiry guarantee exists. Do not assume the two statements mean the same thing.
