## What did the purified shilajit study find?

**A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study reported higher total testosterone, free testosterone and DHEAS after purified shilajit than after placebo.** Participants were healthy men aged 45–55; the regimen was 250 mg twice daily for 90 consecutive days. The abstract reports statistical significance below 0.05 for those comparisons ([Pandit and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26395129/)).

That is relevant human evidence, but its scope is narrower than a promise to restore energy or improve sexual performance. The result concerns the tested purified preparation and the enrolled population. It does not tell a younger athlete, someone taking hormone treatment, or someone buying a different resin what their individual result will be.

The abstract also reports that luteinizing hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone levels were maintained. We do not turn that observation into a claim that shilajit “balances hormones.” That phrase would imply a much broader benefit than the measured finding supports.

The practical limitation is product matching. A package can identify an ingredient without establishing that its contents match a research preparation. Before relying on this paper to justify a purchase, ask what evidence connects the actual product to the study. If that connection is unavailable, the honest conclusion is that product-specific efficacy remains unestablished.

## What did the tongkat ali studies find?

**Tongkat ali has encouraging total-testosterone findings, especially in men starting with low levels, but the evidence is not consistent across populations or outcomes.** A [systematic review and meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9415500/) found a significant pooled effect on total testosterone. Its normal-testosterone subgroup did not show a statistically significant benefit.

That distinction matters more to a buyer than a headline calling tongkat ali “stronger.” Evidence in men with low starting testosterone cannot simply be transferred to everyone with fatigue, a demanding training schedule or an interest in supplements. The review also reported substantial variation between study results and signs of publication bias, which weaken confidence in a simple universal estimate.

A useful example is the [12-week Physta trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8254464/). It analyzed 105 men aged 50–70 with starting testosterone below 300 ng/dL. Participants received 100 mg daily, 200 mg daily or placebo.

Total testosterone differed significantly from placebo at week 12 in the lower-dose group, and at weeks 4, 8 and 12 in the higher-dose group. Free testosterone, however, did not show a significant between-group difference at any visit. Some changes within the treatment groups were significant compared with their own starting values; that is not the same as outperforming placebo.

The study was sponsored by the extract manufacturer, Biotropics Malaysia Berhad, and two authors were employees. Sponsorship does not automatically invalidate results, but it belongs beside the findings when judging how much confidence to place in them. Independent replication with clearly described preparations would make the buying decision easier.

## Why can’t the study numbers identify a winner?

**Separate placebo-controlled studies do not establish which supplement works better than the other.** The shilajit trial enrolled healthy middle-aged men; the Physta trial enrolled older men selected for low testosterone. Different starting conditions alone make a direct ranking questionable.

The preparations, regimens and follow-up schedules also differed. Comparing a percentage from one paper with a percentage from another can produce a precise-looking answer without a fair experiment behind it. Neither cited trial randomized participants between shilajit and tongkat ali.

Use a study-reading checklist instead:

- **Population:** Do the participants resemble the person considering the supplement?
- **Preparation:** Is the product actually the tested extract or purified material?
- **Comparison:** Did the outcome improve versus placebo, or only versus baseline?
- **Outcome:** Was the study measuring a blood marker, symptoms or performance?
- **Uncertainty:** Were the limitations, sponsorship and missing results disclosed?

This approach also avoids confusing “more research” with “better for me.” Tongkat ali’s broader review is useful, but the review itself highlights uncertainty. Shilajit’s controlled result is worth discussing, but it does not fill every product-specific gap.
