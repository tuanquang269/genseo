## Does vaginal odor mean bacterial vaginosis?

**A fishy smell can occur with bacterial vaginosis, but smell alone cannot identify the cause.** The [CDC bacterial vaginosis guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/bv.htm) describes diagnosis using clinical criteria or laboratory testing, rather than a shopper's assessment of odor.

The clinical Amsel approach considers discharge, vaginal pH, cells seen under a microscope, and an amine odor test. Meeting at least three of those four criteria supports a BV diagnosis. The odor test is a clinical assessment, not an instruction to perform a chemical test at home.

There is another reason not to self-diagnose: [trichomoniasis can also cause discharge with a fishy smell](https://www.cdc.gov/trichomoniasis/about/index.html). CDC says symptoms alone cannot diagnose that infection; a healthcare provider uses examination and laboratory testing. Buying a supplement does not answer which condition is present.

## Can an oral probiotic change odor?

**A small study reported improvements, but it tested a specific probiotic alongside antibiotics—not FloraFresh gummies on their own.** That distinction matters more than a front-label phrase such as “vaginal probiotic.”

The [published Unique IS-2 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3460128/) included 40 women in India diagnosed with BV. Both groups received antimicrobial treatment. One group also received oral *Bacillus coagulans* Unique IS-2, identified as ATCC PTA-11748.

Researchers assessed symptoms and clinical findings, including the whiff test for amine odor. They reported a negative whiff test in 16 of 20 participants receiving the probiotic, compared with 9 of 20 controls. Those are counts from this particular study—not a predicted success rate for a product sold today.

The authors acknowledged the small sample and open-trial design. Participants were not blinded, and the study does not separate a supplement-only effect from the treatment context. Its findings cannot tell an individual shopper whether their odor will improve, or how long that would take.

Most importantly for this article, **Unique IS-2 is not SNZ-1969**, the strain identified for FloraFresh. The cited trial did not test the finished FloraFresh product. Presenting its results as evidence that FloraFresh clears odor would misrepresent the research.

## What does CDC guidance mean for shoppers in the US?

**Do not replace prescribed BV treatment with a probiotic supplement.** The [CDC treatment guideline](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/bv.htm) recommends treating women with symptoms and says the probiotic products it reviewed are not supported as adjunctive or replacement BV therapy overall.

That recommendation and an individual small trial answer different questions. A trial can generate a promising research signal without establishing a dependable treatment option across products, populations, and settings. The practical shopping rule is narrower: do not interpret “contains probiotics” as “treats the infection causing my odor.”

CDC also advises that douching might increase relapse risk and notes that no data support douching for treatment or symptom relief. Trying to wash away a persistent smell is not a substitute for evaluation.

If symptoms recur after treatment, contact your clinician rather than assuming the same cause or increasing your supplement intake. CDC notes that persistent or recurrent BV is common and advises returning for evaluation when symptoms recur. Bring the supplement packaging and a list of what you have already used.

## How should you prepare for an odor appointment?

**Write down what changed and ask what testing is appropriate before shopping for a remedy.** A short, factual description helps keep the appointment focused on your actual symptoms rather than a product promise.

Useful notes to bring include:

- When you first noticed the change and whether it has persisted or returned.
- Any accompanying discharge, itching, burning, or discomfort.
- Treatments or supplements already tried, with the packaging if available.
- Whether you are pregnant or could be pregnant.
- Questions about testing for BV, trichomoniasis, or another explanation.

Ask directly: “What does my test show, what treatment do I need, and what should I do if symptoms come back?” For confirmed trichomoniasis, [CDC guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/trichomoniasis/about/index.html) includes partner treatment and follow-up testing. That is another example of care a gummy cannot replace.

You do not need to wait until a supplement trial is finished before seeking evaluation. If you are already using FloraFresh, describe it as a supplement you take—not as a confirmed treatment for the cause of your symptoms.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can vaginal probiotics get rid of a fishy smell?

A fishy smell needs a cause-specific assessment. The small oral probiotic trial discussed here studied Unique IS-2 alongside antimicrobial treatment, not FloraFresh by itself. It does not justify promising odor relief from these gummies. Ask a clinician about testing if the smell is new, persistent, or recurrent.

### Is odor always BV?

No. CDC lists fishy-smelling discharge among possible symptoms of both BV and trichomoniasis. Symptoms overlap, so smell alone cannot distinguish them. A clinician can select appropriate testing and recommend treatment based on the result.

### How long should I wait for FloraFresh to change odor?

The sources reviewed for this article do not establish a FloraFresh odor-relief timeline. Do not use an arbitrary waiting period to postpone evaluation. A study of another strain is not a basis for promising results within days or weeks.

### Can I take a probiotic instead of antibiotics for BV?

Do not substitute a probiotic for prescribed treatment. CDC recommends treating symptomatic BV and does not support the probiotic products it reviewed as replacement therapy. Discuss any supplement you want to add with your clinician; keep taking prescribed medicines as directed unless your prescriber changes the plan.
