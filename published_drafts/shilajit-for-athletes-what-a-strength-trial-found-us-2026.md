Shilajit may help some athletes retain more strength after a fatiguing effort, but one 8-week trial does not prove that every resin increases strength, recovery, or performance. In the US, the study used a specific purified extract in recreationally active men; retail products, including ShilaSource, must be judged by their own label and documentation.

## What did the strength trial actually find?

The 2019 Keller et al. randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial found a narrower result: the higher study dose was associated with a smaller drop in maximal voluntary isometric contraction after a demanding leg-extension fatigue protocol. It was a fatigue-resistance result, not proof of larger muscles, a higher one-repetition maximum, or better competition results. [Read the full open-access study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6364418/).

The study enrolled 63 recreationally active men for eight weeks. Participants received placebo, a lower study dose, or a higher study dose of PrimaVie® shilajit. The exercise test used two sets of 50 maximal bilateral concentric leg extensions at 180°/s, separated by two minutes. That design matters: it tested performance immediately after a prescribed fatigue challenge, not an athlete's entire training cycle.

Among all participants, the reported post-fatigue MVIC decline was 9.1% with the higher study dose versus 15.7% with the lower study dose. The paper reported no significant group difference for decline in concentric peak torque. The result supports a possible effect on maintaining force under this laboratory challenge; it does not establish a universal shilajit dose for athletes.

## Does shilajit increase strength or reduce muscle fatigue?

Shilajit for athletes should be described as a research question, not a guaranteed strength supplement. Keller et al. measured how much force changed after fatigue. They did not show that shilajit directly builds muscle, replaces progressive resistance training, improves every sport, or prevents ordinary post-workout soreness.

The study also measured serum hydroxyproline, an indirect marker related to collagen degradation. The overall groups did not differ on that measure. A subgroup with higher baseline hydroxyproline had a lower value with the higher study dose than with the two comparison conditions, but that finding does not establish better tendon recovery or injury prevention.

The participants were young, healthy men without known cardiovascular, pulmonary, metabolic, or musculoskeletal disease. They were not a representative sample of women, older athletes, elite competitors, injured athletes, or people taking medication. The trial also did not include a structured resistance-training program. Those limits keep the conclusion appropriately narrow.

## When should athletes take shilajit around training?

There is not enough evidence from the Keller trial to name a proven pre-workout or post-workout timing rule. The study assessed daily supplementation over eight weeks and tested participants after the intervention; it did not establish that taking shilajit immediately before lifting produces an acute performance boost.

A practical approach is to follow the product label consistently rather than chasing a precise training-minute window. Track the product, amount directed on its label, training session, sleep, hydration, and any unwanted effects. Change one variable at a time so a training response is not confused with a new supplement, extra caffeine, altered programming, or improved recovery habits.

Athletes should also avoid treating a supplement as a substitute for food, carbohydrate availability, adequate protein, hydration, sleep, and a progressive program. Those fundamentals have a larger role in day-to-day performance than a single small trial can establish for shilajit.
