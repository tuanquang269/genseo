## What did the turkesterone human study find?

**The preliminary experiment did not find significant effects on its measured acute outcomes.** The [published abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40757520/) reports eleven apparently healthy male participants, with samples collected before ingestion and at three and twenty-four hours afterward. These are details of the experiment, not instructions for using a retail supplement.

Researchers assessed insulin-like growth factor-1, resting metabolic rate, and lipid and carbohydrate metabolism. Their analyses did not reveal significant condition, time or interaction effects for those outcomes. The authors describe the findings as preliminary and call for research on longer-term muscle growth. That matters for someone whose goal is a stronger squat or more muscle over a training block. ## What does the DHEA research actually support?

**The longer human trial reviewed here does not support DHEA as a general physical-performance or antiaging solution.** The [randomized, double-blind trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17050889/) followed older adults for two years. It included eighty-seven men and fifty-seven women with specified low hormone measurements at enrollment.

DHEA supplementation increased circulating sulfated DHEA, but changing that blood measurement did not translate into a broad functional benefit. The study reported no significant effect of DHEA on body-composition measurements. Neither hormone intervention altered muscle strength, peak oxygen consumption or insulin sensitivity, and neither improved quality of life.

The abstract also reports site-specific bone-density findings. Those observations should not be erased, but they are not evidence that an otherwise healthy gym-goer should buy DHEA for muscle growth. The authors' overall conclusion was that the interventions lacked physiologically relevant benefits on the principal body-composition, physical-performance, insulin-sensitivity and quality-of-life outcomes.

Population matters as much as duration. These were older participants selected using hormone measurements, not an unrestricted sample of recreational lifters. Their results cannot supply a personalized recommendation for a younger adult, nor can they replace an assessment of a specific medical concern.

The mechanism paper linked earlier studied hepatic cells. It supports identifying DHEA as a sex-hormone precursor, but its laboratory findings are not used here to claim a treatment benefit in people. A cell experiment, a blood-marker change and a better everyday outcome are different kinds of evidence.
