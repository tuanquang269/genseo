## What does NMN research actually show?

**NMN is a precursor to NAD+, and a small human trial found that supplementation increased blood NAD+. That result does not establish that everyone will feel more energetic.** NAD+ participates in metabolic reactions; measuring its concentration is not the same as measuring daily stamina.

A [randomized, double-blind trial in 30 healthy participants](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2022.868640) compared NMN with placebo over 12 weeks. The researchers reported increased whole-blood NAD+ and no obvious physiological or laboratory adverse effects under those study conditions. The trial supports a narrow statement about that intervention and measurement, not a promise of age reversal or lifetime safety.

The distinction matters when reading a product page. “Supports cellular energy” on a bottle is not equivalent to “shown to reduce your afternoon fatigue.” Before borrowing a research result, ask whether the study used the same formulation and measured the outcome you care about. The trial cited here was not a trial of the AgeAura NMN + TMG product.

Another [randomized study in 108 older Japanese adults](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14040755) examined sleep quality, fatigue and physical performance over 12 weeks. Participants were assigned to morning or afternoon NMN or placebo groups. The afternoon NMN group had the largest reported effect sizes for the sit-to-stand test and drowsiness, but the afternoon placebo group also improved in drowsiness, and all groups improved in the sit-to-stand test.

That pattern is more useful than a headline saying NMN “beats fatigue.” It shows why readers should inspect the comparison groups rather than treat every before-and-after improvement as an ingredient effect. It also does not tell a younger adult how quickly they will notice anything.

For practical label-reading rather than a new dosing prescription, see our [NMN + TMG dosage guide](https://wellnessnest.co/blogs/news/nmn-tmg-dosage-how-much-per-day). Keep trial protocols separate from the directions on the product you are considering.

## What does CoQ10 research actually show?

**CoQ10 has human fatigue research, but the small trial reviewed here does not establish a universal benefit or superiority over NMN.** The study population and fatigue task matter as much as the positive result.

In a [double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover study of 17 healthy volunteers](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nut.2007.12.007), researchers tested CoQ10 during a fatigue-inducing physical workload. The higher-dose condition improved selected cycling performance and subjective fatigue outcomes compared with placebo. Each treatment period lasted eight days. This was a small, specific experiment, not a broad test of unexplained everyday tiredness in adults over 40.

A crossover design lets participants experience different conditions, but it does not erase the limits of a small sample or a laboratory task. A result obtained during a controlled cycling workload cannot supply a guaranteed timetable for your workday, household routine or exercise program.

The [National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health's CoQ10 overview](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/coenzyme-q10) also emphasizes that evidence varies by purpose. Its overview does not support treating CoQ10 as a reliable answer for statin-associated muscle pain, and it notes important medication interactions. The page is dated January 2019; it is useful for those cautions, not a claim to summarize every newer trial.

If a clinician has suggested CoQ10 for a specific reason, discuss that reason directly rather than replacing the recommendation with NMN because both appear under an “energy” heading online. This article compares ingredient evidence, not branded CoQ10 products or treatment plans.
